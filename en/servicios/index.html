What gets built here

Six kinds of work, and they are genuinely different from one another.

1 · Bespoke software

An application that does not exist off the shelf because your process is not the standard one. Typically a web application with its own database, roles and permissions, an admin area and an API. Realistic examples: an internal management panel replacing a spreadsheet that three people edit at once, a booking system with rules a generic plugin cannot express, or a client portal.

  • StackTypeScript, Node, React, PostgreSQL or SQLite. Boring on purpose: it has to survive without me.
  • DeliveryDeployed and working, with the repository handed over and a README that lets someone else pick it up.
  • Not thisNative iOS/Android apps and video games are outside what I do well. I will say so.

2 · SaaS from scratch

A product you sell by subscription, which is not the same as an application with a login. A SaaS needs a set of pieces that are boring to build and expensive to get wrong: multi-tenancy (each customer isolated from the others), plans and limits, billing that reconciles with the payment provider, an onboarding that works without you on a call, and the operational side — who gets woken up when it breaks at three in the morning.

  • BillingStripe or Polar, with webhooks that survive a duplicate: charging twice is not a bug you can apologise for.
  • Self-serveSign up, pay and start without a sales call. This studio does not do person-to-person selling and neither should your product need it.
  • Honest scopeA first sellable version, not the roadmap for two years. Anything else is how a SaaS dies before its first invoice.

3 · Chatbots and assistants grounded in your data

Not a chatbot that improvises. An assistant that answers from your documents, your catalogue or your ticket history, and that cites where each answer came from — that is retrieval-augmented generation, RAG. The interesting part is not the model: it is the ingestion (your PDFs, your database, your intranet), the chunking, and the rule that when there is no source it says it does not know instead of inventing.

  • GroundedEvery answer carries its source. Without that, a chatbot is a liability with a friendly face.
  • YoursIt can run against a local model so no document leaves your network. That is a real option here, not a slide.
  • MeasuredA set of questions with known answers, checked on every change. A chatbot nobody measures degrades silently.

4 · AI agents and process automation

An agent is not a chatbot: it is a program that reads a state, decides, acts on real systems and leaves a trace of what it did. This is the part I use on myself every day — the home Raspberry Pi runs an agent that detects its own faults, tests the repair in isolation and rolls it back if it fails. The same shape applies to a business: invoices that arrive by email and end up classified in the right system, incidents that get triaged before a human reads them, reports that assemble themselves.

  • ReversibleEvery action an agent takes has a way back. Automation without an undo is a faster way to break things.
  • ObservableTraces and metrics from day one, so "the agent did something odd" is a question with an answer.
  • With a gateAnything irreversible —money, deletions, outbound messages— stops and asks. Fail-closed, by default.

5 · LLM integration into a product you already have

You do not want a new product; you want the one you have to do something it cannot. Summarise a long thread, extract structured fields from a scanned document, classify what arrives, draft a first reply. The work is mostly not the prompt: it is the cost per request, the latency your interface can tolerate, what happens when the provider is down, and how you tell whether the output is right before it reaches a customer.

  • Cost firstMeasured per request before it ships. An integration that triples your unit cost is a bad feature, however clever.
  • Local optionWhen the volume justifies it, a local model on your own GPU. I maintain a benchmarking tool precisely for that decision.
  • FallbackWhat the product does when the model answers nonsense or does not answer at all, decided on purpose rather than by accident.

6 · Internal tools and dashboards

The least glamorous item on this list and often the one that pays for itself fastest. A panel that pulls together numbers currently living in four places, a CLI that turns a twenty-minute manual routine into one command, an alert that fires before a customer notices. It usually costs a fraction of the other five and removes a recurring, invisible tax on somebody's week.

  • Local-firstRuns on your machine or your server. Two of my published tools have zero dependencies and zero network calls on purpose.
  • SmallDeliberately small scope, so it ships in days and you find out early whether it was the right tool.
  • Yours to changeReadable code with its tests, because the person who ends up modifying it will not be me.

How a project actually runs

The whole methodology, in five steps. Nothing starts until step two is signed off.

1

You describe the problem

In writing, by email, in your own words. No form with fifteen fields and no discovery call to qualify you. If it does not fit here I will tell you in that first reply, and point elsewhere.

2

Closed scope and price

A written proposal: what is included, what is explicitly not, what you receive, on what date and for how much. A fixed figure, agreed before a single line is written. If the scope changes later, that is a new figure — not a surprise on the invoice.

3

Built with agent fleets

This is what makes the timeline possible: several AI agents working in parallel on separate parts, with me directing and reviewing. It is not magic and it is not free of errors — which is precisely why step four exists and is not optional.

4

Verified on the real system

Green tests are not proof. Everything is checked on the thing actually running, with real data, and the checks themselves get audited — I publish a tool whose entire job is catching tests that pass without testing anything. Applied here first.

5

Handover and afterwards

The repository, the deployment, the documentation and the decisions taken along the way — including the ones that turned out badly. Then a support window, and if you never need me again that is a good outcome, not a lost customer.

How it is priced

Per project, closed, and agreed in writing before starting.

Never by the hour

Billing by the hour rewards slowness and punishes exactly the thing that makes this studio work — an agent fleet that compresses weeks into days. You pay for a result, not for time spent.

One figure, decided up front

The figure depends on scope and comes with the proposal in step two, before any commitment. Whether it is worth it is your call, made with the number in front of you rather than after three invoices.

Published products have public prices

If what you want is one of the tools rather than a project, the price is on its page: Veredicto at $19 per repository and Trace at $39. Self-serve, no call needed.

No lock-in in the price

The delivered code is yours, on your infrastructure, with no licence tying it to me. Nothing here is priced on the assumption that leaving is expensive for you.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Do you work with companies outside Málaga?

Yes, and it is the normal case. The studio is based in Málaga but every project runs remotely, so a client in Madrid, Barcelona, Seville or any other province gets exactly the same process: written proposal, closed price, delivery and handover. Being in the same city changes nothing about the work — only the option of meeting in person.

What does a project cost?

It depends on scope, and the figure arrives with the written proposal before any commitment — never as an open hourly rate. What can be said in advance is the shape: one closed price, agreed before the work starts, and a change of scope becomes a new figure rather than a surprise. The published products do have public prices: Veredicto at $19 per repository, Trace at $39.

How long does it take, if you use AI agents?

Faster than a comparable team, but the honest answer is that it depends on what has to be verified, not on how fast the code appears. Agents produce code quickly and also produce plausible mistakes quickly; the time goes into checking on the real system. A small internal tool can be days. A SaaS with billing is weeks, because getting money wrong is not recoverable.

Does my data or my code end up in an AI provider?

It is decided with you, in writing, before starting — it is not a footnote. When it matters, the whole thing runs against local models on your own hardware and nothing leaves your network; that is a real capability here, not a promise, and I maintain the benchmarking tool used to choose which local model is worth it.

Who owns the code when it is finished?

Yours. The repository is handed over with its documentation and its tests, deployed on your infrastructure, with no licence tying it to me and no platform you cannot leave. If tomorrow you want another studio to carry on, they can — that is the point.

Is this an agency, a consultancy or a freelancer?

A one-person software studio: a self-employed engineer directing fleets of AI agents, invoicing from Spain. Not an agency, because there is no sales layer and no subcontracting chain. Not a consultancy in the report-writing sense either — what gets delivered is working software, not a deck.
